\subsection{Experiment Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

% -----------------------------

% -----------------------------
\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.}
To characterize the robustness of the learned local planner under stochastic disturbances, we evaluate our method and baselines on a standardized benchmark suite.
We instantiate three specific difficulty levels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Easy (6 Obstacles)}: Sparse environment.
    \item \textbf{Medium (8 Obstacles)}: Moderate clutter.
    \item \textbf{Hard (10 Obstacles)}: Dense, highly dynamic environment requiring precision maneuvering.
\end{enumerate}
Each method is evaluated on \textbf{100} randomly generated episodes per difficulty level (Total 300 episodes).

\paragraph{Environment instantiation.}
Each episode is generated by randomizing flow sources (vortices) and static/dynamic obstacles.
Dynamic obstacles spawn within specific regions and move with velocity profiles influenced by the local current.
A square boundary is enforced; leaving the valid region is counted as an \emph{out-of-bounds} failure.

\paragraph{Control and rollout settings.}
The simulation time step is $\delta t = 0.1$\,s, and the robot executes each action for $N=10$ integration steps, resulting in a control period of $\Delta t=1.0$\,s (1 Hz).
An episode terminates when the agent reaches the goal, collides, or exceeds the maximum step budget of 1000 steps (relaxed from previous settings to fairly evaluate slower planners like APF). The maximum linear acceleration is $0.4$\,m/s$^2$ and the maximum speed is $2.0$\,m/s.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We report four metrics following common practice in local navigation benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Success rate:} fraction of episodes that reach the goal without collision or out-of-bounds.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-bounds rate:} fraction of episodes ending due to boundary violation.
    \item \textbf{Average time:} mean time-to-goal computed \emph{only} over successful episodes.
    \item \textbf{Average energy:} an action-effort proxy defined as the sum of control command magnitudes along the trajectory: $E = \sum_{t} (|a_t| + |w_t|) \cdot \|\mathbf{v}_t\| \Delta t$, computed over successful episodes.
\end{itemize}

% (Moved from: Risk-Sensitive IQN Variants)

\paragraph{Adaptive risk sensitivity.}
We evaluate our Distributional-Risk Planner instantiated with \textbf{Implicit Quantile Networks (IQN)} under both \textbf{fixed} and \textbf{adaptive} risk sensitivity.
Concretely, we adopt a CVaR-style distortion with threshold $\phi \in (0,1]$ and test fixed settings $\phi \in \{0.25,0.50,0.75,1.0\}$ to span a spectrum from strongly tail-focused (conservative) to risk-neutral (greedy).

In addition, we implement an \textbf{Adaptive IQN} with a distance-adaptive threshold that modulates risk sensitivity based on proximity to the nearest obstacle. The dynamic quantile threshold $\phi$ is computed as:
\begin{equation}
\phi = \max\left( \phi_{\min}, \min\left( \frac{d_{\min}}{d_0}, 1.0 \right) \right)
\label{eq:adaptive_phi}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\min} = \min(d(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{X}_O))$ is the distance to the nearest obstacle sensed by the sonar, $d_0=8.0$\,m is the safety activation radius, and $\phi_{\min}=0.3$ is a lower bound to prevent overly conservative freezing behaviors. This formulation ensures that the agent behaves nearly greedily ($\phi \approx 1.0$) in open water for efficiency, but smoothly transitions to a risk-averse mode ($\phi \to 0.3$) as collision threats materialize.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare our method against a comprehensive suite of navigation approaches, including classical reactive planners, value-based DRL, and state-of-the-art policy gradient methods.

\paragraph{Classical planners.}
We include (i) \textbf{Artificial Potential Fields (APF)}, which uses attractive/repulsive forces for navigation. While theoretically sound, APF often suffers from local minima in dynamic flow fields, leading to inefficient oscillatory paths.
(ii) \textbf{Bug Algorithm (BA)}, a heuristic boundary-following strategy.

\paragraph{Deep RL baselines.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DQN / DDQN / D3QN}: Standard value-based methods representing the baseline performance of Q-learning without distributional perspective.
    \item \textbf{PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)}: A state-of-the-art on-policy algorithm known for its stability and ease of tuning. PPO serves as a strong, high-performance baseline often used in continuous control tasks.
    \item \textbf{Greedy IQN}: An ablation of our method with fixed risk-neutral distortion ($\phi=1.0$) to isolate the contribution of the adaptive risk mechanism.
\end{itemize}

All learning-based agents share identical observation encoders and are trained under the same curriculum schedule.

\paragraph{Effect of adaptive risk sensitivity.}
Our improved risk adaptation mechanism is critical for balancing safety and efficiency. We observe that fixed risk settings (e.g., conservative $\phi=0.25$) achieve high success but suffer from excessive path deviation and energy waste. Conversely, greedy settings ($\phi=1.0$) are fast but crash frequently. By modulating $\phi$ based on obstacle proximity, our approach achieves the best of both worlds.

\paragraph{Energy efficiency.}
Adaptive IQN maintains minimal energy consumption, ensuring sustainable long-duration operations. While classical methods like APF can yield low-energy paths in simple cases, they suffer from high variance and inefficiency (spiking to 24\,J, average 18.87\,J) in complex, dynamic flows.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis: Robustness in Extreme Conditions}
To provide deeper insight into the behavioral differences between risk-neutral and risk-aware policies, we analyze a specific challenging scenario (Seed 0) involving interacting dynamic obstacles. Fig.~\ref{fig:traj_seq} illustrates the trajectory evolution of five different planners.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/trajectory_sequence_refined_seed0.png}
  \caption{Qualitative comparison of trajectory evolution in a complex dynamic environment (Seed 0).
  (a) Start ($T=0.0$s): All agents initialize in a flow field with approaching dynamic obstacles.
  (b) Early Stage ($T=12.0$s): The risk-neutral \textbf{PPO} agent (dashed blue) optimizes for expected return and fails to account for the worst-case movement of the dynamic obstacles, resulting in a collision.
  (c) Late Stage ($T=25.0$s): The \textbf{Adaptive IQN} agent (solid red) proactively identifies the high-risk region and executes a wide evasive maneuver.
  (d) Final ($T=38.0$s): Adaptive IQN successfully reaches the goal. In contrast, reactive planners like \textbf{APF} (dotted gray) get trapped in local minima due to the complex flow-obstacle interaction, failing to progress.}
  \label{fig:traj_seq}
\end{figure*}

As shown in the time sequence, the environment presents a "trap" where the optimal path requires an early deviation to avoid oncoming obstacles. 
The \textbf{PPO} agent, driving straight towards the goal to maximize instantaneous reward, collides at $T\approx11$s. This failure highlights the limitation of expectation-based optimization in dynamic environments—it underestimates the tail risk of rare collision events.
In contrast, \textbf{Adaptive IQN} leverages its distributional value estimation. By assigning higher weight to the lower quantiles of the return distribution (representing collision risks), it "senses" the potential danger earlier and chooses a safer, albeit slightly longer, path around the obstacle cluster.
Other baselines like \textbf{APF} survive but fail to reach the goal, getting stuck in a local minimum caused by the opposing flow and potential field repulsion. 
This qualitative example demonstrates that our method effectively dominates the trade-off by being safer than PPO and more efficient than APF.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

\paragraph{Ablation logic.}
To analyze the contribution of each design component, we conduct ablation experiments from two aspects.
First, we perform a component-level ablation by progressively removing key modules in the proposed method.
Second, we compare different fixed risk thresholds with the proposed adaptive risk mechanism.
All ablation variants use the same training and evaluation setup as the full model.

We first study the effect of three key components: risk-sensitive decision-making (RS), adaptive risk modulation (AR), and the energy-aware term (ET).

\paragraph{Analysis of component ablation.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_components} shows that all three components contribute to the final performance.
Comparing \textbf{V1} (which approximates a standard DQN with energy penalties) and \textbf{V2} shows that introducing risk-sensitive decision-making (using a fixed moderate risk level $\phi=0.5$) improves the overall success rate in cluttered environments (91\% $\to$ 96\%).
Comparing \textbf{V2} and \textbf{V4} indicates that adaptive risk modulation provides a better balance between success and efficiency than using a fixed risk level (improving success to 100\% while reducing average time from 23.05s to 21.50s).
Comparing \textbf{V3} and \textbf{V4} further shows that the energy-aware term helps reduce unnecessary control effort and improves overall energy efficiency, reducing average energy consumption from 19.85\,J to 14.95\,J without compromising the 100\% success rate.

\paragraph{Analysis of risk-threshold settings.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation_phi} reveals a clear safety--efficiency trade-off under fixed risk thresholds.
Smaller $\phi$ leads to more conservative behavior and usually improves success rate, but it may also increase travel time and energy consumption.
In contrast, larger $\phi$ improves efficiency but tends to reduce robustness in dense scenarios.
The proposed adaptive strategy achieves a better overall balance by remaining efficient in open space while becoming more cautious near nearby obstacles.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The ablation study supports three main observations.
First, risk-sensitive decision-making is important for robust navigation in complex dynamic environments.
Second, adaptive risk modulation is more effective than any single fixed threshold.
Third, the energy-aware term contributes to lower control effort and better energy efficiency.
